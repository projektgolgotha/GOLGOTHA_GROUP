# SEM-106 — The Twelve Prophets

**Version:** 0.1 faculty-review draft, 31 October 2026  
**Pathway:** ECS-300 Ethiopian Canon Studies, proposed 81-book delivery sequence  
**Format:** Proposed noncredit prophetic-literature survey  
**Planning estimate:** 36 study hours across four learning blocks  
**Prerequisite:** SEM-000 and SEM-101; SEM-105 recommended or concurrent by faculty approval  
**Status:** Not faculty approved; exact editions, readings, historical assumptions, teaching sources, and assessment rules remain under review

## Course description

Study Hosea, Amos, Micah, Joel, Obadiah, Jonah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi as twelve distinct prophetic voices within one coordinated survey. Learners will produce an overview record for every book and then complete focused comparisons on justice, worship, covenant, the nations, violence, mercy, exile, restoration, and the “day of the Lord.”

The course maps to **OT-33–OT-44**. The adopted syllabus should use **The Twelve Prophets** rather than “minor” to avoid implying that the works are unimportant. Faculty may retain “Minor Prophets” as a conventional library or search term when it is explained.

## Proposed alignment

Primary program outcomes: **PLO-1 Canon literacy, PLO-2 Interpretation, PLO-3 Ethiopian context, PLO-4 Research integrity, PLO-5 Teaching, and PLO-6 Reflective practice**.

## Proposed course learning outcomes

After completing the adopted course, a learner should be able to:

1. Locate and summarize the distinctive literary and theological contribution of all twelve books using traceable references.
2. Compare two prophetic arguments while accounting for audience, genre, historical proposal, and literary context.
3. Analyze how justice, worship, covenant, mercy, violence, judgment, and restoration function differently across the collection.
4. Distinguish the primary text’s rhetoric from a modern political or predictive application.
5. Develop a focused teaching activity that states a measurable outcome, handles difficult material responsibly, and cites its evidence.
6. Revise a comparison or teaching decision after feedback and explain the improvement.

## Proposed course rhythm

For each book:

1. record the edition and reading scope;
2. identify genre, speaker, audience, and literary movement;
3. note one central argument and one unresolved question;
4. engage one approved teaching source or secondary reading;
5. add the book to the twelve-entry overview matrix;
6. revise at least one interpretation after discussion or feedback.

## Proposed block activities

### Block 1 — Justice without sloganizing

Compare one passage from Amos or Micah with one passage from Hosea or Joel. Identify the specific practice, audience, rhetorical strategy, and relationship between worship and public responsibility. Explain why quoting a famous verse without its surrounding argument can distort the text.

**Submission:** four unit records and a 700–900-word comparison with one plausible alternative interpretation.

### Block 2 — Reading enemies and mercy

Read Obadiah, Jonah, and Nahum as distinct works. Compare their portrayal of nations, judgment, mercy, or violence. Avoid turning a historical enemy label into a license to target a contemporary ethnic, religious, or political group.

**Submission:** three unit records and a 600–800-word ethical-interpretation memorandum that identifies the limits of modern transfer.

### Block 3 — Lament and faithful waiting

Map the dialogue or movement in Habakkuk and compare it with Zephaniah’s judgment and restoration language. Identify what is complaint, response, vision, prayer, promise, or later application.

**Submission:** two unit records, a structural map, and a 500–700-word interpretation of how lament and trust coexist.

### Block 4 — Rebuilding and symbolic hope

Compare Haggai’s rebuilding concerns, Zechariah’s symbolic visions, and Malachi’s worship/community critique. For Zechariah, learners must distinguish imagery explicitly interpreted in the text from later proposals.

**Teaching task:** prepare a 10-minute lesson and one-page facilitator guide using two books from different blocks. Include one measurable outcome, one source-based comparison, one discussion question, one check of understanding, and a statement limiting the application.

**Submission:** three unit records, completed twelve-entry matrix, teaching demonstration, feedback, and a 200–300-word revision reflection.

## Proposed study-check domains

The study check should evaluate whether a learner can:

- name and order all twelve registry units used in this course;
- distinguish complete-text and selected-passage assignments;
- identify speaker, audience, genre, and rhetorical setting;
- compare a justice or worship theme without reducing it to a slogan;
- explain why Jonah and Nahum should not be collapsed into one treatment of enemy nations;
- distinguish textual symbolism from unsupported modern prediction;
- identify a fair alternative interpretation;
- explain why a historical enemy label cannot be transferred automatically to a living group.

The final question bank should be created only after faculty approves the readings and edition.

## Instruction and learner support

The course contains violence, sexualized metaphor, war, national judgment, trauma, and apocalyptic imagery. Faculty should approve content notices, discussion norms, accessible alternatives, and an anti-targeting rule. Learners should not be required to disclose personal trauma or political affiliation.

The instructor should intervene when an interpretation dehumanizes a living community, bypasses textual evidence, or claims predictive certainty beyond the assigned text. Intervention should focus on the published academic and ethical criteria, not viewpoint punishment.
