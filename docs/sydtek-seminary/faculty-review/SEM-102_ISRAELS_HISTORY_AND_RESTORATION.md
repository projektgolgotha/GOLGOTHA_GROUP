# SEM-102 — Israel’s History and Restoration

**Version:** 0.1 faculty-review draft, 31 October 2026  
**Pathway:** ECS-300 Ethiopian Canon Studies, proposed 81-book delivery sequence  
**Format:** Proposed noncredit survey  
**Planning estimate:** 44 study hours across five learning blocks  
**Prerequisite:** SEM-000 and SEM-101, or documented equivalent preparation  
**Status:** Not faculty approved; primary editions, exact readings, teaching sources, instructor, and assessment rules remain subject to review

## Course description

Study the formation, fracture, exile, restoration, and retelling of Israel’s communal history through the Seminary registry’s historical units. The course compares Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Ezra–Nehemiah, diaspora narratives, and later historical memory while requiring learners to distinguish a primary text’s account from a modern historical reconstruction.

The course maps to **OT-09, OT-10, OT-11, OT-12, OT-15, OT-16, OT-17, OT-18, OT-19, and OT-46**. Several numbered entries contain more than one component. Faculty must approve the exact edition and reading extent of **Ezra II and Ezra Sutuel**, the additions or textual extent used for **Esther**, and **Joseph ben Gorion / Josippon** before those units can be treated as assessed coverage.

## Proposed alignment

Primary program outcomes: **PLO-1 Canon literacy, PLO-2 Interpretation, PLO-3 Ethiopian context, PLO-4 Research integrity, PLO-5 Teaching, and PLO-6 Reflective practice**.

## Proposed course learning outcomes

After completing the adopted course, a learner should be able to:

1. Account for every SEM-102 registry unit and each grouped component in a traceable reading record.
2. Compare parallel or related historical narratives without assuming that retellings have identical purposes.
3. Distinguish literary presentation, theological interpretation, source-based historical proposal, and contemporary application.
4. Evaluate how leadership, worship, exile, return, minority identity, and communal memory are framed across different genres.
5. Produce a text-identity dossier for an ambiguous or less familiar work, clearly separating established evidence from uncertainty.
6. Design a short teaching activity that uses a historical text responsibly and includes a measurable learner outcome.

### Reading-scope rule

Every learner record must identify whether the assignment used selected passages, a complete work, or an approved excerpt. The course must not imply complete-book reading where only selected passages were assigned.

## Proposed block activities

### Block 1 — Competing models of leadership

Create a comparison chart for 1 Samuel 8, 1 Samuel 16, 2 Samuel 7, and 2 Samuel 11–12. Identify narrator description, direct speech, institutional concern, and the consequence of leadership decisions.

**Submission:** separate component worksheets for 1 Samuel and 2 Samuel plus a 500–700-word analysis of how the narrative both establishes and critiques kingship.

### Block 2 — Explaining national collapse

Trace one explanatory thread across 1 Kings and 2 Kings: worship, covenant, political division, prophetic speech, injustice, or reform. Distinguish what the narrative says from a modern reconstruction of the period.

**Submission:** separate component worksheets for 1 Kings and 2 Kings and a source-checked timeline with no more than ten events. Every historical claim beyond the assigned passages requires a citation.

### Block 3 — The work of retelling

Compare a parallel episode in Samuel/Kings and Chronicles. Record what is shared, omitted, emphasized, or reframed. Do not reduce the comparison to “one account is right and the other is wrong”; explain the literary evidence and possible audience function.

**Submission:** worksheets for 1 and 2 Chronicles plus an 800–1,000-word historiographic comparison.

### Block 4 — Restoration and contested community

Study rebuilding, public reading, economic justice, intermarriage, and communal boundary decisions. Learners should identify both the text’s explicit reasoning and the ethical questions that arise when a modern community applies the account.

**Submission:** component worksheets for Ezra and Nehemiah. For OT-16, submit a **text-identity dossier** rather than pretending coverage: proposed titles, edition evidence, numbering problem, available translations, unresolved questions, and a recommendation for faculty action.

### Block 5 — Minority identity and memory

Compare how Tobit, Judith, and Esther portray danger, agency, prayer, providence, courage, violence, and communal survival. Add Josippon only after faculty approves the edition and selected scope.

**Submission:** unit worksheets for Tobit, Judith, Esther, and—if approved—Josippon; a 700–900-word comparison that fairly represents at least two interpretive approaches; and a 10-minute teaching activity with a one-page guide.

## Proposed study-check domains

The study check should require learners to:

- identify when one registry number contains multiple books;
- distinguish a narrative’s theological explanation from a modern historical claim;
- identify a valid parallel-text comparison;
- explain why Ezra II/Sutuel cannot be filled by Ezra–Nehemiah alone;
- explain why a general Josephus source does not automatically establish Josippon coverage;
- recognize the difference between narrative description and a universal command;
- document an edition and reading scope;
- represent a difficult ethical interpretation fairly.

A question bank and answer rationale should be created only after the faculty approves the readings.

## Instruction and learner support

An assessed pilot should include at least one instructor-led discussion for each block, a weekly question route, and substantive feedback within seven calendar days. Because several readings address violence, persecution, communal boundary, and contested identity, the instructor should publish discussion norms and a pastoral/support referral process before the course opens.

The course should be split into two courses if pilot workload or faculty judgment shows that ten registry entries and the grouped components cannot be taught and assessed responsibly in 44 hours.
