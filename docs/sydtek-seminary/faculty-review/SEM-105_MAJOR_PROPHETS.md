# SEM-105 — Major Prophets

**Version:** 0.1 faculty-review draft, 31 October 2026  
**Pathway:** ECS-300 Ethiopian Canon Studies, proposed 81-book delivery sequence  
**Format:** Proposed noncredit prophetic-literature survey  
**Planning estimate:** 40 study hours across four learning blocks  
**Prerequisite:** SEM-000 and SEM-101; SEM-102 recommended  
**Status:** Not faculty approved; exact corpus boundaries, editions, readings, historical bibliography, and assessment rules remain under review

## Course description

Interpret judgment, lament, hope, symbolic action, exile, restoration, and apocalyptic vision through Isaiah, the Jeremiah corpus, Ezekiel, and the Daniel corpus. The course emphasizes literary context, genre, source control, and explicit accounting for texts grouped under a single registry entry.

The course maps to **OT-29–OT-32**. The registry treats Jeremiah and Daniel as corpora rather than simple single-component units. The adopted course must explicitly determine the place of **Lamentations, Baruch, Rest of Jeremiah, Susanna, and Rest of Daniel** in the selected edition and reading plan. A Jeremiah or Daniel playlist alone does not establish coverage of those additional components.

## Proposed alignment

Primary program outcomes: **PLO-1 Canon literacy, PLO-2 Interpretation, PLO-3 Ethiopian context, PLO-4 Research integrity, PLO-5 Teaching, and PLO-6 Reflective practice**.

## Proposed course learning outcomes

After completing the adopted course, a learner should be able to:

1. Account for Isaiah, every approved Jeremiah-corpus component, Ezekiel, and every approved Daniel-corpus component in a traceable record.
2. Interpret prophetic speech, poetry, narrative, lament, symbolic action, and apocalyptic vision with attention to literary setting and genre.
3. Trace a theme of judgment, exile, restoration, covenant, leadership, justice, or hope across at least two prophetic corpora without erasing differences.
4. Distinguish the primary text’s claims from a historical reconstruction, later theological interpretation, and contemporary prediction.
5. Evaluate a teaching source for textual accuracy, corpus coverage, responsible use of imagery, and public-claim limits.
6. Design and revise a teaching activity that avoids sensationalism and states what the assigned passage can and cannot support.

### Corpus-accounting rule

A learner may not receive full unit completion for OT-30 or OT-32 unless every component required by the adopted edition and syllabus has its own reading/evidence record. Where faculty holds a component pending evidence, the course must state that limitation publicly and in the learner record.

## Proposed block activities

### Block 1 — Read the literary movement

Create a thematic and literary map of the assigned Isaiah passages. Identify shifts in speaker, audience, setting, judgment, consolation, servant imagery, worship, and future hope. Compare one judgment passage with one restoration passage without treating either as the whole book.

**Submission:** Isaiah unit worksheet, literary map, and a 700–900-word close-reading essay.

### Block 2 — Build the Jeremiah-corpus dossier

Document the selected edition’s contents and order. Create separate component records for Jeremiah, Lamentations, Baruch, and Rest of Jeremiah. If a component has not been established, record the gap instead of substituting a related text.

Compare Jeremiah 29–31 with one approved lament or Baruch passage. Distinguish historical setting, literary voice, theological interpretation, and modern application.

**Submission:** corpus dossier, component records, and an 800–1,000-word comparison.

### Block 3 — Interpret symbol with context

Select one Ezekiel symbolic action or vision and trace its explanation within the surrounding chapters. Identify what is described, what is interpreted within the text, and what remains a later interpretive proposal.

**Submission:** Ezekiel worksheet, annotated visual or structural map, and a 600–800-word interpretation that explicitly limits its contemporary application.

### Block 4 — Apocalyptic reasoning without sensationalism

Compare a narrative of faithful practice under empire with one symbolic vision. Identify genre, narrative setting, symbols, explicit interpretation, unresolved questions, and the difference between the text’s horizon and a modern prediction.

**Submission:** Daniel-corpus component records; a 900–1,200-word genre-aware interpretation; and a 10-minute teaching demonstration with a one-page guide and revision note.

## Proposed study-check domains

The study check should evaluate whether a learner can:

- identify a registry unit with multiple required components;
- distinguish prophecy, lament, narrative, symbolic action, and apocalypse;
- locate a claim within its immediate literary setting;
- distinguish an explicit interpretation in the text from a modern proposal;
- explain why Jeremiah material cannot automatically cover Lamentations, Baruch, or Rest of Jeremiah;
- explain why Daniel material cannot automatically cover Susanna or other additions;
- document an edition and corpus order;
- state limits on contemporary prediction and application.

The final question bank should be written only after faculty approves the readings and corpus boundaries.

## Instruction and learner support

The course includes war, exile, sexualized metaphor, communal trauma, destruction, coercive power, and apocalyptic imagery. Faculty should review content notices, accessible alternatives, pastoral-risk language, and discussion norms. The instructor must not use fear, claims of secret certainty, or political targeting as a substitute for textual reasoning.

Learners should be permitted to analyze a difficult image or passage without being required to endorse a specific modern prediction. Grading should evaluate the published outcomes and evidence rather than agreement with an unstated eschatological position.
