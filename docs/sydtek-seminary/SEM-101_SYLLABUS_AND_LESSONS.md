# SEM-101 — Torah and Israel's Beginnings

**Version:** 0.1, 30 October 2026. **Pathway:** ECS-300 Ethiopian Canon Studies, proposed delivery sequence. **Format:** noncredit survey. **Planning estimate:** 40 study hours across four learning blocks. **Prerequisite:** SEM-000 or an equivalent orientation review. This authored syllabus awaits academic adoption, reading-edition approval, and instructor assignment.

## Course description

Explore creation, covenant, liberation, holiness, land, leadership, and belonging through Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, Joshua, Judges, and Ruth. Read selected primary passages, examine a teaching source, and build a short analysis for each book. The emphasis is on explaining what a passage says, how you reached your interpretation, and how a responsible application follows from the evidence.

This course maps to canon entries **OT-01–OT-08**. The complete reading of each book is a useful extension, but the required survey readings below are selected passages.

## Learning outcomes

After completing the course, learners should be able to:

1. Locate each of the eight books in the adopted canon registry and produce a traceable reading record for each one.
2. Compare at least three passages on a shared theme while accounting for literary genre and immediate context.
3. Distinguish textual observations, historical proposals, theological interpretations, and contemporary applications.
4. Explain one ethical or interpretive difficulty fairly, including the limits of the available evidence.
5. Design and deliver a short teaching session supported by primary passages and appropriately identified secondary sources.

## Block 1 — Guided opening lesson

### Read closely: Genesis 1:1–2:3

Before consulting a commentary, read the passage twice. On your first reading, note its sequence and repeated language. On the second, identify transitions, the portrayal of humanity, and the function of the ending. Use references to the passage rather than long copied quotations.

Write three observations and label each one with a verse reference. Then write one interpretation that draws on at least two observations. Ask: would another reader be able to follow the reasoning from the passage to my interpretation?

### Compare carefully: Genesis 12:1–9 and Exodus 19:1–8

For each passage identify the speaker, addressee, promise or instruction, and response. Draft a comparison table. Explain one connection and one difference without assuming the passages are interchangeable. Identify any historical question your comparison raises and what further evidence would help answer it.

### Discussion prompt

A teacher says, “The same covenant theme appears in both passages, so their settings do not matter.” Write a respectful response in 150–200 words. Use one textual detail from each passage and explain how context changes the teaching decision.

### Submit

Complete a unit worksheet for Genesis and one for Exodus. Each worksheet includes the selected reading ranges, source record, a 250–350-word analysis, one unresolved question, and a 75–100-word application with its reasoning. Revise after feedback.

## Block 2 — Worship and community

Read the prescribed Leviticus passages as a set before extracting a single rule. Compare Leviticus 19 with Numbers 27:1–11. Identify the kind of material, who is affected, and how the passage presents responsibility or a request for judgment.

**Assignment:** Explain in 400–500 words how a teacher can discuss an ancient legal or ritual text responsibly with a contemporary audience. Support the explanation with two passages and distinguish the text's setting from your proposed application. Submit the Leviticus and Numbers worksheets.

**Discussion:** Describe a point at which your application moves beyond what the passage explicitly states. What additional theological or historical reasoning do you need to make that move clear?

## Block 3 — Memory and interpretation

Compare the uses of memory and decision in Deuteronomy 6, Deuteronomy 30, and Joshua 24. For Joshua, also read the surrounding context of the assigned passages before discussing leadership or land.

**Assignment:** Produce a 700–900-word comparative interpretation using three passages from at least two books studied so far. Include a clear claim, close textual evidence, a plausible alternative interpretation, and a response to that alternative. Identify the edition used and one suitable secondary source.

**Ethical question:** What happens if a modern teacher transfers the language of an ancient narrative directly onto a contemporary group? Identify the interpretive steps required before making such an application. The assignment is to reason carefully about interpretation, not to target a living community.

Submit Deuteronomy and Joshua worksheets.

## Block 4 — Narrative and teaching

Compare the literary setting and leadership patterns in the assigned Judges passages with the whole narrative of Ruth. Attend to how characters speak, act, and receive descriptions from the narrator. Distinguish narrative description from an instruction to imitate every action.

**Teaching task:** Prepare a 10-minute lesson with a one-page facilitator guide. Address a specific adult learning group, state one measurable outcome, use two primary passages, include one discussion question, and design a brief check of understanding. Cite the source of every historical claim that is not evident from the passage. Record or deliver the lesson through the agreed route.

Submit Judges and Ruth worksheets and a 200-word reflection on the feedback you received. Explain one revision you made and why.

## Reading and interpretation study check

Choose the strongest answer. The passages, not recalled lecture wording, are the reference point.

1. Which pair gives the clearest starting point for comparing promise and communal covenant responsibilities? A: Genesis 12:1–9 and Exodus 19:1–8. B: Genesis 1 alone. C: Judges 21:25 alone. D: Two modern summaries without primary passages.
2. When an assigned passage is part of a law or ritual collection, the first interpretive move is to: A: assume it directly describes every modern setting; B: identify its literary and communal context; C: replace it with a narrative; D: infer its date from its popularity.
3. Why read Ruth as a complete narrative? A: A shorter book needs no interpretation. B: Only its final verse matters. C: The sequence and relationships can qualify a reading of an isolated event. D: Its genre makes context irrelevant.
4. What makes a comparison of Deuteronomy 6 and Joshua 24 persuasive? A: Similar words without a claim. B: A description of the teacher's preferences. C: A shared illustration with no references. D: Specific textual observations linked to a reasoned similarity and difference.
5. A narrative depicts a character's action. Which conclusion requires further argument? A: The action appears in the narrative. B: Every contemporary reader is commanded to imitate it. C: The surrounding scene provides context. D: Character speech and narrator description can differ.
6. Which reading note is evidence rather than a conclusion? A: “This proves my entire doctrine.” B: “The author agrees with every modern practice I prefer.” C: “The speaker addresses a named group at this point in the passage.” D: “No alternative reading is possible.”
7. What should a source-based historical claim include? A: An identifiable source and the basis for the claim. B: Only the phrase “scholars say.” C: Only a confident delivery. D: An unrelated quotation.
8. What is the best response to a plausible alternative interpretation? A: Omit it. B: Attribute bad motives to its reader. C: Mention it without examining evidence. D: Represent it fairly and explain where your reading agrees or differs.
9. What does a useful learning outcome for a 10-minute lesson ask the learner to do? A: “Know everything about Exodus.” B: “Compare two features of the assigned passages using references.” C: “Appreciate my teaching.” D: “Complete all biblical research.”
10. Which revision note shows learning? A: “I changed the font.” B: “I deleted the criticism.” C: “I revised my claim because the passage's context did not support its original scope.” D: “I retained the conclusion without checking the source.”

**Instructor/self-study answer key:** 1 A; 2 B; 3 C; 4 D; 5 B; 6 C; 7 A; 8 D; 9 B; 10 C. For graded use, separate the key from the learner submission and require a brief explanation for two selected answers.
